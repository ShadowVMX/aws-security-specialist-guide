Security operations, identity and compliance

The heaviest domain on the exam alongside infrastructure. Its four tasks share one underlying question: which capability is needed to satisfy a requirement, not which button turns it on. Nobody asks you to configure Sentinel here: you are asked to decide whether the problem is solved with Sentinel, with Defender XDR, with both, or with neither.

Quiz (100 questions)

What this exam takes for granted

SC-100 assumes you arrive with administrator-level knowledge: it takes for granted that you know how the pieces work and only asks you which one you pick. This section is that base, because without it half a dozen design decisions in this domain look arbitrary when they are actually derivable.

What a token is, and why that forces CAE

When somebody authenticates, Entra ID does not hand them "access": it hands them an access token, a signed document carrying claims — who you are, for which application, with which permissions, until when. The application validates it by checking the signature, without asking Entra ID again. That is what makes the cloud fast and scalable, and it is also its problem: the token is good until it expires, typically around an hour, even if you disable the account, change the password or the user's risk spikes in the meantime.

Alongside it travels a refresh token, much longer lived, used to obtain new access tokens without reauthenticating. That is why "revoking the session" is not instant by default: you wait for the access token to expire and for the refresh token to be rejected.

With that, CAE stops being a name and becomes obvious Continuous access evaluation establishes a channel between Entra ID and the applications that support it — Exchange, SharePoint, Teams, Graph — so that a critical event — account disabled, password changed, risk elevated, leaving the compliant network — makes the application reject a token that had not yet expired. That is the whole idea, and it explains why the exam asks for it whenever the stem says "must lose access immediately".

How conditional access evaluates, precisely

A policy has two halves. The assignments decide what it applies to: users and groups, applications or actions, and conditions — user risk, sign-in risk, device platform, location, client app, device state. The controls decide what happens: grant while requiring something (MFA, compliant device, protected app, authentication strength) or block; plus session controls, which limit what can be done once inside.

  • Every applicable policy is evaluated and all of them must be satisfied. There is no "first match wins". If two policies require different things, both are enforced.
  • Block always beats any grant.
  • Exclusions beat inclusions. Hence protecting the emergency account by excluding it, and hence a mistake in an exclusion being so dangerous.
  • Within one policy, several grant controls can be required all or any. That is the system's only either/or.
  • Whatever no policy covers, goes through. That is why you validate a design by hunting for the gap, not by reading what is written.

Kerberos in one paragraph, and the four attacks stop being names

In a domain you do not send your password to every server. You authenticate once against a domain controller and receive a ticket-granting ticket (TGT), encrypted with the key of a special account called krbtgt. When you want to use a service, you present the TGT and ask for a service ticket for that service, identified by its SPN — the name it is registered under in the directory. That ticket is encrypted with the service account's password.

AttackWhat it exploits, exactly
KerberoastingAny authenticated user can request the ticket for a service with an SPN. That ticket is encrypted with the service account's password, so they take it away and crack it offline, with no failed attempts and no lockout. That is why the defence is not "a longer password" but the password ceasing to be human: a gMSA generates a 240-character one and rotates it itself.
Golden TicketWith the krbtgt key you can forge valid TGTs for any user, including one that does not exist, with whatever permissions you like. Since the TGT is not validated against anything else, the domain believes them. Hence rotating it twice: the account keeps the current key and the previous one, so a single rotation would leave the old one still valid.
DCSyncDomain controllers replicate to each other. Whoever holds replication permissions can request password hashes as if they were another controller, without touching a single machine. Hence the defence being to audit who holds that permission.
Pass-the-hash / pass-the-ticketKerberos and NTLM do not need the cleartext password: the hash or the ticket is enough, and those sit in the memory of whichever machine somebody signed in on. That is why the tiered model forbids using domain credentials on lower-trust machines: it is not bureaucracy, it is where they get harvested.

PIM: eligible versus active

A role assignment has two states. Eligible means you can activate it, but you do not have it: your token does not carry that permission. Active means you actually have it. On activation, PIM can require MFA, justification and approval, and it creates a real assignment with an expiry; when that expires, it removes it. All of it is logged.

What this implies for the exam: being eligible for Global Administrator does not appear in your token. An attacker who steals your session does not inherit the roles you had not activated. That is PIM's real value, and it is why "reduce standing permissions" and "shorten the window" are the same idea.

What a Sentinel analytics rule actually is

When the syllabus says "writing detections", this is what it means: an analytics rule is a query in KQL — the Azure Monitor query language — that runs on a fixed interval over the workspace's tables and creates an alert if it returns rows. Alerts are grouped into incidents. A rule also carries its MITRE ATT&CK mapping, and that is where the coverage report comes from.

The design consequence, which is what gets asked: if the data is not ingested into a table, the rule cannot see it. That is why "connect the source" always precedes "detect", and why the decision about what gets ingested is simultaneously a detection decision and a cost decision.

The workspace tiers, which are the cost decision

TierWhat it allowsWhen
AnalyticsFull query and analytics rules over that table.Whatever feeds detections and hunting.
Basic / auxiliaryLimited query, far cheaper per gigabyte.High volume and low analytic value: raw network flows, verbose proxy logs.
Long-term retentionData is kept, and restored or searched when needed.A regulatory obligation or an old investigation.

With that, the classic answer — "move the high-volume logs to a cheaper tier and promote only what feeds a rule" — stops being a memorised trick and derives itself.

Design, Evaluate, Specify: how this exam asks

All fourteen SC-100 tasks begin with one of those three verbs. Not one of them says Configure. The practical consequence is that almost every option in a question is technically possible: what separates them is whether they satisfy every constraint in the stem.

What the stem saysWhat it is measuring
"without deploying agents", "with no infrastructure to maintain"Whether you know the agentless or managed variant of the control.
"the security team must not hold standing permissions"PIM, just-in-time access, access reviews.
"there is an on-premises AD DS that is not going away"That the solution has to be hybrid, not cloud only.
"must comply with standard X" followed by "with minimal operational effort"That a template or initiative already exists and does not need writing.
"multiple clouds" / "AWS and GCP too"Azure Arc, Defender for Cloud multicloud connectors, and its CIEM capabilities.
The reflex worth training Read the last sentence of the stem first. That is usually where the constraint that eliminates two of the four options lives — cost, operational effort, no agents, or the requirement that it also work outside Azure. Read the options afterwards, with that constraint already in mind.

XDR and SIEM: who does what, and when you need both (2.1.1)

This is the most profitable confusion in the domain to clear up, because the exam mines it again and again.

Microsoft Defender XDRMicrosoft Sentinel
What it isDetection and response over the Microsoft estate: identities, endpoints, email, cloud apps, servers.SIEM and SOAR over any source, including what is not Microsoft.
Where the signal comes fromIts own sensors. Nothing to connect: you turn it on.Connectors. Anything you do not connect does not exist for it.
CorrelationAutomatic across its workloads, into already-assembled incidents.Whatever you write: analytics rules in KQL.
RetentionFixed per product.Whatever you pay for, up to years, across different cost tiers.
What only it can doAutomatic response on the endpoint, the identity and the mailbox.Ingest a third-party firewall, a mainframe or a custom application log.

That yields the criterion: XDR covers the Microsoft estate out of the box; the moment the requirement names a foreign source — network appliances, another cloud, a line-of-business application, multi-year retention — Sentinel comes in. And the most common answer in a large-enterprise scenario is both, with Defender XDR connected into Sentinel and the SOC working in the unified Defender portal.

The trap "We need to correlate endpoint, identity and email alerts" does not on its own justify a SIEM: Defender XDR already does that with nothing connected. What justifies the SIEM is a source appearing from outside that family. If you pick Sentinel where XDR was enough, you have added ingestion cost and rule-writing work to get what already came finished.

Centralised logging and auditing (2.1.2)

Three pieces the exam mixes deliberately, because they answer different questions:

  • Microsoft Purview Audit — the record of user and admin activity in Microsoft 365 and Entra: who read a mailbox, who exported, who changed a role. It answers "what did this person do?". Its Premium tier extends retention and adds high-value events.
  • Sentinel — the analytics store. This is where you ingest what has to be correlated and what has to raise an alert.
  • Azure Monitor / Log Analytics — the platform underneath. The design decision is rarely "which product" but how many workspaces and with which table tiers, because that is where the cost comes from.

The classic design mistake: ingesting everything into the analytics tier. High-volume, low-analytic-value logs — raw network flows, verbose proxy logs — belong in a cheaper tier or long-term storage, and you promote only what feeds a rule.

Monitoring hybrid and multicloud environments (2.1.3)

The question is almost always "we have servers outside Azure and/or workloads in AWS or GCP". The pattern:

What has to be reachedWhat reaches it
On-premises or other-cloud servers, as if they were Azure resourcesAzure Arc: projects them into the Azure control plane and enables Policy, Defender for Servers and Machine Configuration over them.
Posture and recommendations for AWS and GCP accountsThe Defender for Cloud multicloud connectors, which read the other cloud's configuration agentlessly.
The logs from those environments, for correlationSentinel connectors.
Arc is not a log agent Azure Arc exists to govern the resource from Azure. Getting its events into the SIEM is a separate decision. In a scenario with both needs, the answer carries both pieces.

SOAR: automation rules versus playbooks (2.1.4)

Sentinel has two mechanisms and the exam distinguishes which one applies:

Automation rulePlaybook (Logic App)
What forGoverning the incident inside Sentinel: assign, tag, change severity, close, chain.Acting outside: open a ticket, isolate a machine, notify in Teams, call an API.
Cost and latencyNative, immediate.Logic Apps execution, with its own billing.
When it is preferredWhenever the action stays inside the incident.When another system has to be touched.

Rule of thumb: if the stem says "automatically close known false positives" or "assign by incident type", that is an automation rule. If it says "create a ticket in ServiceNow" or "disable the account", that is a playbook — and if the action is on the endpoint or the identity, often you do not even need one: Defender XDR automatic response already ships it.

Designing the flows: response, hunting and incident management (2.1.5)

Three activities that are not done the same way, and that the exam keeps apart:

  • Incident response — reactive, triggered by an alert. Designed in phases (prepare, detect, contain, eradicate, recover, lessons learned) and with who decides at each one.
  • Threat hunting — proactive, with no prior alert: it starts from a hypothesis ("if an attacker were doing this, what trace would they leave?") and runs as queries over data that is already there. It needs sufficient retention, and that is its link to the earlier cost decision.
  • Incident management — the process: severity, owner, escalation, communication and closure.
The confusion they are fishing for A stem asking to "proactively look for suspicious activity that no rule has detected" describes hunting, not detection. Adding more analytics rules does not solve it: what it needs is access to historical data and queries, not another alert.

Evaluating detection coverage with MITRE ATT&CK (2.1.6)

ATT&CK is the map you use to answer "what would we not find out about?". You cross active rules against the matrix techniques, and whatever stays blank is the gap. The exam expects you to know there are three matrices and that they are not interchangeable:

MatrixWhen it is the right one
EnterpriseWindows, Linux, macOS, containers, identity and SaaS. The default.
MobileThreats against iOS and Android.
ICSIndustrial control systems. The one that goes with Defender for IoT in a plant or OT scenario.

Agent identities: Microsoft Entra Agent ID (2.2.1)

New content and highly askable, because it solves a problem that until now was solved badly. An AI agent acting on its own needs an identity: without one, it either gets the user's — inheriting everything they have — or a shared application credential nobody reviews.

Entra Agent ID gives the agent its own directory identity, with a lifecycle, an owner and an audit trail. And the part that matters for the exam: because it is a first-class identity, Conditional Access policies apply to it exactly as they do to a person, so the agent stays bounded by the resources, conditions and risk you decide. For the same reason it falls under identity governance: it has an owner, it can be put through access reviews, and least privilege applies to it with roles scoped to the resource, not a broad role "so we don't have to adjust it".

The principle underneath "Give the agent its own identity and treat it like any other principal." If an option proposes that the agent use the invoking user's credentials, or a shared secret in code, it is the wrong one even though it would work.

Access to SaaS, PaaS, IaaS, hybrid and multicloud (2.2.2)

The answer is almost never a single product but a combination across three layers, and the stem usually asks for all three without saying so:

  • Identity — who they are and under what conditions they get in: Entra ID as the single provider, Conditional Access, phishing-resistant MFA.
  • Network — how the request arrives: Entra Private Access for internal, Entra Internet Access for egress, private endpoints for PaaS.
  • Application — what they can do inside: Defender for Cloud Apps for SaaS, with session policies that block downloads on an unmanaged device.

Entra ID in hybrid and multicloud environments (2.2.3)

MethodWhere the password is validatedWhen the stems pick it
Password hash syncIn the cloud, against a hash of the hash.The default. Survives an on-premises outage and enables leaked-credential detection.
Pass-through authenticationAgainst on-premises AD DS, through agents.When policy requires validation to happen on-premises.
Federation (AD FS)In your own federation infrastructure.Requirements only AD FS covers. These days the exam usually points the other way: away from AD FS.
Resilience With PTA or federation, if the on-premises environment goes down nobody signs in to the cloud either. A stem asking that "users keep authenticating even if the data centre is unavailable" is asking for password hash sync, even if only as a fallback.

External identities: B2B and decentralised (2.2.4)

  • B2B collaboration — the partner comes in as a guest in your tenant, with their own identity. Your Conditional Access policies apply to them.
  • B2B direct connect — mutual trust between tenants without creating guests; it is what backs Teams shared channels.
  • Cross-tenant access settings — the governance piece: which tenants, which users and with what assurances (for example, accepting the MFA the user already performed in their home tenant).
  • Decentralised identity / Verified ID — verifiable credentials the person holds themselves. It shows up when the stem wants to prove an attribute — that someone is an employee of a given company, that they hold a given qualification — without storing their data or depending on the other party's directory.

Conditional Access, continuous evaluation and risk (2.2.5 / 2.2.6)

Conditional Access is the Zero Trust decision engine: every request is evaluated against signals — user, device, location, application, risk — and is granted, strengthened or blocked.

PieceWhat problem it solves
Continuous access evaluation (CAE)A token staying valid after the user has been disabled or their risk has risen. With CAE, revocation applies almost immediately, without waiting for expiry.
User risk / sign-in risk
Entra ID Protection		What computes that risk is Microsoft Entra ID Protection, with its detections — leaked credentials, impossible travel, anonymous IP address, threat actor activity. Telling "this account looks compromised" apart from "this particular sign-in is odd". The first calls for a secure password change; the second, for MFA.
Protected actionsRequiring recent, strong authentication for specific high-impact operations — modifying the Conditional Access policies themselves, for instance — even if the session was already open.
Authentication strengthRequiring a specific method, not "MFA" in the abstract: for administrators, phishing-resistant (FIDO2, Windows Hello for Business, certificate).
Report-only mode and What IfPublishing a policy without enforcing it: it is evaluated on every real sign-in and the outcome is logged, so you see who it would block before blocking anyone. What If does the same for one specific case, even before you publish it.
Validating against Zero Trust (2.2.6) A Conditional Access policy that only looks at user and application is not Zero Trust. The device signals (compliance, Entra join) and the risk signals are missing. And so is what almost nobody remembers: an excluded emergency access account, because one badly written rule can lock out every administrator at once.

Specifying AD DS hardening (2.2.7)

AD DS still sits underneath almost every enterprise, and the exam treats it as what it is: the attacker's real objective. What you are expected to be able to specify:

  • Tiered model (tier 0/1/2): domain administration credentials are never used on lower-tier machines. That is what cuts lateral movement.
  • Privileged accounts and groups reduced to a minimum, with time-bound membership.
  • Windows LAPS so the local administrator password is unique and rotated on every machine — without it, one compromised machine opens all the others.
  • Defender for Identity on the domain controllers, which is what detects DCSync, Golden Ticket, kerberoasting and reconnaissance enumeration.

Managing secrets, keys and certificates (2.2.8)

NeedService
Application secrets, keys and certificates, with rotation and Azure integrationAzure Key Vault (Standard, or Premium with HSM backing)
Exclusive control of the module, single tenant, FIPS 140-3 Level 3Azure Key Vault Managed HSM
The application holding no credential at allManaged identities: there is no secret to rotate because there is no secret
Issuing and renewing internal certificatesKey Vault integrated with the CA, with automatic renewal
The order of preference Managed identity > secret in Key Vault > secret in the application configuration. If an option removes the secret instead of guarding it better, it is usually the right one.

The enterprise access model (2.3.1)

It is the successor to the classic tier model and the reference Microsoft expects you to design privilege delegation against. The idea to retain: the planes are separated — control, management and data/workload — and an identity from a higher plane is never exposed on a lower one. Applied to the cloud, that means whoever administers Entra ID does not use that same account to administer a subscription, nor that one to sign in to a machine.

PIM, entitlement management and access reviews (2.3.2 / 2.3.6)

PieceWhat it eliminates
Privileged Identity ManagementStanding permission. The role is activated for a limited time, with justification, approval and MFA, and it is logged.
Entitlement managementManual onboarding: access packages with request, approval and expiry policy, which also serve external users.
Access reviewsThe permission that outlives the reason it was granted: someone recertifies periodically and whatever is not confirmed is removed.
The distinction they ask about PIM solves how long a role is active. Access reviews solve whether that person should still have it. They do not substitute for each other: a PIM-eligible role nobody has reviewed in two years is still a governance problem.

Evaluating AD DS governance and resilience (2.3.3)

Here the exam wants you to recognise the attacks by name and know what stops them:

AttackWhat it abusesWhat cuts it
Pass-the-hash / pass-the-ticketCredentials left in memory on a lower-tier machine.Tiered model, Credential Guard, dedicated workstations.
DCSyncReplication permissions granted too widely.Auditing who holds those permissions; detection with Defender for Identity.

Secure administration of cloud tenants (2.3.4)

An Entra ID tenant is the root of trust for everything else, and what you are asked to design is how it is administered without becoming the single point of failure: administrators in PIM and not permanent, dedicated workstations, emergency access accounts excluded from Conditional Access and watched with an alert if they are used, and separation between whoever administers identity and whoever administers the workloads. In SaaS and multicloud scenarios, the same discipline extends to each provider's console.

CIEM: cloud infrastructure entitlements (2.3.5)

The problem it solves: in any cloud, most granted permissions are never used, and every one of them is attack surface. Microsoft's answer is the CIEM capabilities of the Defender CSPM plan in Defender for Cloud: they analyse identity configuration and actual usage across Azure, AWS and GCP and return concrete recommendations on inactive identities, over-provisioned permissions and insecure identity settings, so you can trim to real usage.

Careful with the older product Until recently this was Microsoft Entra Permissions Management, a separate product with its "permission creep index". Microsoft retired it on 1 November 2025 and pointed customers at Defender CSPM. It still shows up in older study material, so it is worth recognising the name and knowing the current answer is Defender for Cloud.
How they word it "We need to see, across all three clouds, which identities hold permissions they do not use." That is CIEM. The distinction they are after outlives the product change: resource posture looks at how the storage account or the machine is configured; permission posture looks at what each identity can do and what it actually does.

Privileged access workstations (2.3.7)

A PAW is a dedicated, hardened machine you administer from, stripped of everything that introduces risk: email, general browsing, arbitrary software. The reasoning the exam expects: there is no point protecting the account if the machine it is used from is exposed, because the attacker does not need the password — the machine where it is typed is enough. For remote access into those environments, the modern variant is a service like Azure Bastion with Entra ID and Conditional Access in front, instead of published RDP.

Translating a regulatory requirement into controls (2.4.1)

This is the most "architect" task in the domain and the one most often failed, because the right answer is rarely a product. The sequence the exam expects:

  1. Identify the specific obligation in the framework, not the whole framework.
  2. Translate it into a verifiable technical or organisational control.
  3. Choose the mechanism that implements it and, above all, the one that evidences it.
  4. Define how it is demonstrated to an auditor repeatably.
Enforcing and demonstrating are not the same Azure Policy enforces and prevents drift. Defender for Cloud and Purview evidence compliance against a framework. A stem asking to "demonstrate to the auditor" is not solved with a policy: it is solved with the report.

Microsoft Purview for compliance requirements (2.4.2)

  • Classification and sensitivity labels — discover where the regulated data lives and mark it, with protection that travels with the file.
  • Data loss prevention — stop it leaving through email, endpoint or SaaS.
  • Data lifecycle management and retention — keep what the regulation requires kept and delete what it requires deleted.
  • Insider risk management and information barriers — the insider threat and separation-of-duties angle.
  • Purview Audit — the evidence of who did what.
  • Compliance Manager — the piece that ties all of the above to the regulation.

Purview Compliance Manager deserves its own paragraph because it is where compliance is run as a programme. It ships assessment templates per regulation — GDPR, ISO 27001, NIST, HIPAA and, now in the syllabus, AI governance ones — breaks each regulation into improvement actions with an owner and a status, reuses shared controls across regulations, and computes a compliance score weighted by each action's risk. It covers Microsoft 365 and resources in other clouds too.

Compliance Manager versus the Defender for Cloud dashboard Both talk about compliance and the exam crosses them. Compliance Manager belongs to Purview and runs the programme: it includes the non-technical actions — policy, process, training — that no configuration tool can assess. The Defender for Cloud regulatory compliance dashboard belongs to Azure and evidences the real technical state of resources against a standard's controls. "Demonstrate how the resources are configured" is Defender for Cloud; "run and evidence the whole compliance programme" is Compliance Manager. Large scenarios carry both.

Azure Policy for security and compliance (2.4.3)

EffectWhat it is designed for
DenyPreventing the non-compliant resource from existing at all. The preventive control.
AuditSeeing the size of the problem without breaking anything. The realistic first step in an environment with resources already deployed.
DeployIfNotExists / ModifyRemediating: deploy the missing configuration or add the setting. Needs a managed identity.
InitiativesGrouping dozens of policies under a regulatory framework and assigning them to a whole scope.
The order matters Putting Deny in up front on a subscription with existing resources does not fix them — it only blocks new ones — and it usually breaks deployments. The pattern the exam is after is Audit first to measure, remediation for what exists, Deny afterwards so it does not come back.

Defender for Cloud to validate regulatory compliance (2.4.4)

The Defender for Cloud regulatory compliance dashboard maps your resources against standards — MCSB by default, plus whatever you add: ISO 27001, PCI DSS, NIST, CIS — and shows, control by control, what is met and what is not, in Azure and also in connected AWS and GCP accounts. It is the tool the continuous evidence comes from; Secure Score, by contrast, is a relative improvement metric, not a compliance report.

AI governance, risk and compliance (2.4.1 / 2.4.2)

The syllabus places AI inside compliance, not only inside technical security, and it is worth keeping that apart because they are different questions. Beyond protecting the service and the data, the organisation has to govern what it does with AI:

ObligationHow it is covered
Knowing which AI systems exist and how risky they areA use-case inventory with a risk classification, and discovery of unapproved AI with Defender for Cloud Apps.
Demonstrating compliance against an AI regulationThe AI governance assessment templates in Purview Compliance Manager, with their improvement actions and owners.
Governing the data going in and outClassification and labels over the corpus, and DLP over the prompt channel.
Being able to audit and investigate usagePurview Audit, with retention and eDiscovery over the interactions.
Detecting misuse by peopleInsider risk management, which has AI-usage indicators of its own.
Making sure the output causes no harmContent filters and human review where the impact demands it.
AI security and AI governance are not the same Protecting the service — identity, network, keys — is security. Answering "which AI systems do we have, how risky are they, who is answerable for each and how do we prove it to an auditor?" is governance, and there the answer lives in Purview, not in Defender. A stem naming an AI regulation and asking for periodic evidence is asking for Compliance Manager.

Common traps in this domain

Sentinel where Defender XDR was enough Correlating endpoint, identity, email and Microsoft cloud app signals is already what XDR does. The SIEM comes in when a foreign source or long retention appears.
Secure Score as a compliance report Secure Score measures improvement; the regulatory compliance dashboard measures conformance against a standard. They are not interchangeable.
PIM instead of access reviews Shortening the activation window does not answer whether that person should still be eligible. Many scenarios need both.
Forgetting the emergency access account Any Conditional Access design that does not exclude break-glass accounts is a total lockout waiting to happen.
Azure Arc treated as a log connector Arc governs the resource from Azure; getting its events into the SIEM is a separate decision.
