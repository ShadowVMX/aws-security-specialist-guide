Designing solutions that align with best practices and priorities

The least technical domain and the one most people get wrong, precisely because its questions are not answered by knowing a product. What is asked here is strategy: what gets protected first, which framework applies, how a business recovers, and how AI gets adopted without opening a new front. The wrong options are usually technically impeccable and strategically useless.

What this exam takes for granted

This domain is about strategy, but it rests on three ideas that are rarely spelled out and that decide whole questions.

Shared responsibility: who answers for what, by service model

What you have to protect changes with the service model, and that split sits underneath many answers:

ResponsibilityOn-premisesIaaSPaaSSaaS
Data centre, physical network, hardwareYoursProvider'sProvider'sProvider's
Operating system, patching, disk encryptionYoursYoursProvider'sProvider's
Network and application configurationYoursYoursYoursShared
Identities, access and dataYoursYoursYoursYours

The row that never changes is the last one: identity and data are always yours, in every model. That is why the exam insists so heavily on classification, labels, conditional access and permission governance, and why an option saying "the provider protects that" about data is almost always the wrong one.

Defence in depth, and what survives of it under Zero Trust

The classic idea is to stack independent controls — perimeter, network, host, application, data — so that one failing is not the system failing. Zero Trust does not replace it: what it denies is one specific premise of that architecture, that being inside the perimeter is a sign of trust. The layers remain; what disappears is the safe zone.

That is why a modern design keeps segmentation and encryption — layers — and adds verification on every request. An option proposing to trust the internal network again is wrong; so is one proposing to remove layers "because we have Zero Trust now".

RPO and RTO: what each measures and what design each forces

Two numbers the business sets, pointing at different mechanisms, and confusing them is the classic error of this task.

RPO — recovery point objectiveRTO — recovery time objective
What it measuresHow much data you accept losing, measured backwards in time.How much time you accept being down.
What it depends onThe frequency of backup or replication.The speed of restoring and returning to service.
What design it forcesRPO in hours: frequent backups. RPO in minutes: continuous replication.RTO in days: restore from backup. RTO in minutes: a secondary environment already standing.

The consequence that gets asked: a demanding RPO and a demanding RTO are not solved by the same piece. You can have continuous replication — a low RPO — and still take a day to come back up if nobody prepared and tested the procedure. That is why the exam separates "backup" from "tested recovery plan".

Who answers for what, model by model

The first two rows never change colour in any column: identity and data are always yours, wherever the workload runs.

The five frameworks, kept apart

This domain names five Microsoft frameworks and the exam constantly sets them against each other. Knowing which question each one answers settles half the questions on its own.

FrameworkWhat it gives youWhen it is the answer
MCRA
Cybersecurity reference architectures		The map: which security capabilities exist, how they fit together and which Microsoft product covers each."What are we not covering?" "How do these pieces fit?"
MCSB
Microsoft Cloud Security Benchmark		The concrete, measurable controls, mapped to CIS and NIST, evaluated automatically by Defender for Cloud."Is this configured properly?" "Give me a baseline."
CAF
Cloud Adoption Framework		The adoption journey: strategy, plan, ready, adopt, govern and manage, with security woven in."How do we organise adoption?" "How do we govern this at scale?"
WAF
Well-Architected Framework		The trade-offs for one workload between security, reliability, cost, operations and performance."Review this workload's design." "What are we sacrificing?"
SAF
Security Adoption Framework		The security transformation programme: where to start, in what order, who does what and how progress is measured, split across disciplines — access, operations, asset protection, governance and innovation."How do we take this from strategy to a plan with owners and dates?"

The confusion to avoid with the last one: the SAF does not compete with the CAF, it complements it. The CAF orders the whole cloud adoption; the SAF orders security modernisation as a programme in its own right, aimed at moving the organisation from where it is to a Zero Trust model. When the stem talks about a security programme with phases, executive sponsorship and owners per discipline, it is SAF.

Antipatterns: the exam asks about these by name too Microsoft publishes not only best practices but what it sees fail. The ones that come up most: security as a gate at the end rather than spread across the lifecycle; trusting the internal network as a safe zone; treating security as a project that ends rather than a programme; buying tools without covering capabilities — the gap the MCRA exposes; and chasing a score instead of reducing risk. Recognising the antipattern is often what eliminates two options at once.
The distinction that comes up most CAF looks at the organisation and its cloud adoption; Well-Architected looks at one specific workload. A stem about management groups, subscriptions and governance is CAF; one asking whether an application is well designed is Well-Architected.

From business goal to security priority (1.1.1)

The exam's first task also sets its tone: the security strategy does not come from the threat catalogue, it comes from what the business needs to keep running. The path it expects:

  1. Identify the critical processes and the assets they depend on.
  2. Agree with the business what it is prepared to lose: RPO — how much data — and RTO — how much time.
  3. Identify the threats putting those assets at risk, not every threat that exists.
  4. Prioritise controls by business impact, not by technical severity.
  5. Measure and revisit, because both the business and the threats change.
RPO and RTO are not security's to set The business sets them, and the technical design follows: a daily backup does not meet a one-hour RPO, however well it is run. A stem that hands you an RPO or an RTO is handing you the constraint that eliminates half the options.

Business continuity and disaster recovery (1.1.2)

NeedPiece
Backing up machines, databases and files, in Azure, on-premises and other cloudsAzure Backup, with the relevant agent for what sits outside
Continuous replication to stand back up in another regionAzure Site Recovery, with a tested recovery plan
Stopping an attacker with permissions from deleting the backupsImmutable backups, soft delete and MFA-protected deletion on the vault
Whoever administers the workload not administering its backupSeparation of duties between operations and backup
Knowing it worksPeriodic test restores and drills, not just green backups
A backup that can be deleted is not a backup This is the point the exam chases in any ransomware scenario: an attacker who gets administrator credentials goes for the backups first. Without immutability, soft delete and separated credentials, the recovery plan is paper.

Ransomware mitigation (1.1.3)

Microsoft orders this defence in three phases and the exam expects you to know which order they come in, because the order is the answer:

PhaseGoalPieces
1. Prepare to recoverSo paying is never the only way out.Immutable, isolated backups, tested restore, AD forest recovery plan.
2. Limit the damageSo one compromised machine does not open the organisation.Privileged access — PIM, dedicated workstations, LAPS, tiered model — segmentation, least privilege.
3. Make entry harderClose the usual doors.Phishing-resistant MFA, patching, email protection, attack surface reduction, EDR.
The order is counter-intuitive, which is why they ask The temptation is to start with prevention. Microsoft puts recovery first because it is the only thing that guarantees the business survives even when prevention fails, and privileged access second because that is what turns an incident into a catastrophe. If a stem asks for "the most urgent" against ransomware, look there before the antivirus.

Evaluating security update solutions (1.1.4)

What needs updatingWith what
Servers in Azure, on Arc or in another cloudAzure Update Manager, with periodic assessment and maintenance windows
Windows clients and managed applicationsIntune, with update policies and deployment rings
Knowing what is missing and how risky it isDefender Vulnerability Management, prioritising by exploitability and exposure
What cannot be patchedDocumented compensating mitigation: isolation, virtual patching, planned retirement

The design the exam expects is not "patch everything immediately" but deployment rings — a pilot group, then production in phases — windows agreed with the business, and a documented fast lane for vulnerabilities being actively exploited.

Designing with the reference architectures (1.2.1)

The MCRA is the capability map: identity, security operations, endpoint protection, infrastructure, application, data, OT and AI security, with what Microsoft offers in each box and how it connects to the rest. It gets used two ways in the exam: to find the gap — which capability has nobody assigned to it — and to justify a choice — why this piece and not another, given what already exists.

Insider, external and supply chain threats (1.2.2)

TypeWhat characterises itControls
ExternalSomebody without legitimate access tries to get it.Strong identity, reduced surface, detection and response.
InsiderThe access is legitimate; the use is not.Insider risk management, DLP, least privilege, separation of duties, information barriers.
Supply chainIt comes in through something you already trust: a dependency, a vendor, a tool.Composition analysis, artefact provenance and signing, third-party access governance, vendor assessment.
Why the insider threat is different No access control stops it, because the access is correct. What addresses it is looking at behaviour rather than permissions, and that is where insider risk management comes in. An option answering "an employee leaving with the customer book" with "strengthen MFA" is answering a different threat.

AI solutions aligned with the MCSB (1.2.3)

New content. The idea being tested is that an AI workload is not a separate category: the same benchmark controls apply as to any other — identity, network, data protection, logging, posture, response — plus its own on top. The typical design mistake is treating it as an experiment outside normal governance.

MCSB controlHow it applies to the AI workload
Identity and accessEntra ID and managed identities; no local keys; RBAC per service.
Network securityPrivate endpoint and public access disabled.
Data protectionCorpus classification, customer-managed keys, control over what gets logged.
Logging and detectionTraces into the SIEM and the Defender for Cloud plan for AI services.
Posture and vulnerability managementThe workload enters Defender for Cloud like any other.
AI-specificContent filters and shields against prompt injection.

The Zero Trust adoption framework (1.2.4)

Three principles, six pillars, and an exam that asks about both. The principles are what you validate a design against:

  • Verify explicitly — every request is evaluated with all available signals; a prior authentication is not trusted.
  • Use least privilege access — just-in-time, just-enough-access, adaptive protection.
  • Assume breach — segment, encrypt, verify end to end and design detection assuming somebody is already inside.

The pillars are where it gets rolled out: identities, endpoints, applications, data, infrastructure and networks, all governed by visibility, automation and orchestration.

Zero Trust is neither a product nor a project that ends If an option proposes "implementing Zero Trust" by buying a tool, it is the wrong one. It is a model adopted pillar by pillar, and the adoption framework exists precisely to give the order and the milestones.

A secure AI adoption strategy (1.3.1)

The other new point in the syllabus, and the one closest to a real customer conversation. The strategy is not about picking a model, it is about setting the boundaries before the organisation starts on its own:

  • Governance and use cases — who approves what AI may be used for, with which data and with what level of human review.
  • Prepare the data first — classify, label and fix oversharing before connecting anything, because an assistant spreads whatever was already shared wrongly.
  • Identity for what acts — agents get their own identity, with an owner, least privilege and governance.
  • Discover unapproved AI — what teams already use on their own is discovered with Defender for Cloud Apps and then sanctioned or replaced.
  • The risks specific to AI — prompt injection, unreliable output, leakage in the prompt: addressed with filters, human review where impact demands it, and DLP over that channel.
The order the exam expects Governance and data before deployment. A stem asking "what do we do before giving Copilot to the whole organisation" is answered with classifying and fixing permissions, not with configuring the assistant or training the users.

The piece the exam wants you to name from Well-Architected is the trade-off: hardening security almost always costs money, performance or operational convenience, and the mature design is the one that says so instead of pretending otherwise.

Here the exam gets concrete: given a gap, what does the framework recommend. The recurring pairings:

Azure landing zones (1.3.4)

A landing zone is an environment prepared in advance so that whatever gets deployed inside is born compliant, rather than audited afterwards. What it brings out of the box, and what the exam wants you to recognise:

  • A management group hierarchy with the policies assigned at the top, so they reach future subscriptions.
  • Separate platform subscriptions — identity, management, connectivity — from the workload ones.
  • Networking with hub and spoke, hybrid connectivity and controlled egress.
  • Identity and RBAC defined, with privileged access from the start.
  • Centralised logging and Defender plans enabled by policy.
  • Everything as code, so creating a new one is repeatable.
Why the scope is the management group It is the answer to "so it applies to subscriptions that do not exist yet". Assigning per subscription means remembering every time somebody creates one, and that forgetting is exactly what the landing zone removes.

A DevSecOps process aligned with the CAF (1.3.5)

The underlying idea: security stops being a gate at the end and spreads across the lifecycle, with automatic controls that do not depend on anybody remembering. In the exam it is recognised by where each control sits — modelling at design, scanning in the code and the pull request, dependencies at build, policy at deployment, detection at runtime — and by two governance decisions: that the pipeline holds a federated identity instead of secrets, and that a control's failure breaks the build rather than raising a warning nobody reads.

Common traps in this domain

Confusing CAF with Well-Architected CAF governs the organisation and its adoption; Well-Architected reviews one workload and its trade-offs.
Starting the ransomware plan with prevention Microsoft's order is recovery, privileged access, then entry. What is urgent is that the business survives the failure.
Backups the attacker can delete Without immutability, soft delete and separated credentials, the recovery plan does not exist.
Treating Zero Trust as a product It is a model adopted pillar by pillar and validated against three principles, not something you buy.
Answering the insider threat with more authentication The access is already legitimate. What applies is looking at behaviour: insider risk management and DLP.
Deploying AI before fixing the data An assistant inherits the source's permissions. Classifying and fixing oversharing comes first.
