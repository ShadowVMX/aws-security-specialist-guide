Security solutions for infrastructure

The other maximum-weight domain. Four tasks that work from the outside in: first knowing what state you are in — posture — and then protecting whatever does the work: servers and clients, managed services, containers, AI, and how traffic gets in and out. Almost every question is won by knowing which product answers which question, not how it is configured.

What this exam takes for granted

This is the domain that assumes the most platform mechanics. Without them, half a dozen answers look like memorisation when they actually derive from how Azure is built.

Private endpoint versus service endpoint, under the hood

A PaaS service — a storage account, a database — is born with a public name resolving to a public address. The two options the exam sets against each other do different things with that:

  • A service endpoint does not change the destination: the resource keeps its public address. What changes is the source: traffic leaves over the Azure backbone and arrives identified as coming from your subnet, so the resource's firewall can allow "this virtual network". There is still a public door, just with a guest list.
  • A private endpoint creates a real network interface inside your subnet, with a private IP address of yours, and a private DNS zone that makes the resource's public name resolve to that private IP. The resource becomes reachable by private address, and with public access disabled it stops existing for the internet.
That is why the answer changes with the requirement "Traffic must not go out to the internet" is met by both. "The resource must only be reachable from the virtual network" is met only by the private endpoint, because it is the only one that removes the public door. And there is a detail the exam exploits: with a private endpoint you must sort out DNS, because if the client keeps resolving to the public IP the private link is never used.

Why posture is agentless and workload protection is not

Every Azure resource sits behind a common control plane, Azure Resource Manager, which knows how each one is configured. Posture simply reads that over the API: that is why CSPM installs nothing, why it works the same over AWS and GCP by assuming a role in the account, and why its answer is always about configuration.

Workload protection needs to see what happens inside: processes on a server, queries against a database, content being uploaded. None of that is in the control plane, so a sensor is required — Defender for Endpoint on the server, scanning at upload time, the cluster node sensor. Hence the line separating the two products, and hence "without agents" being a constraint that always pushes towards posture.

What Azure Arc actually does

Arc installs an agent on the machine that registers it as an Azure Resource Manager resource, with its own identifier, resource group and managed identity. From that moment the machine is, as far as Azure is concerned, just another resource: RBAC, tags, policies and management groups apply to it. What gets added on top — Defender for Endpoint, the Azure Monitor agent, Machine Configuration — are extensions Azure deploys through that agent.

This explains two exam answers at once: why Arc enables Azure Policy over outside servers — Policy acts on ARM resources, and Arc turns them into one — and why Arc does not take logs to the SIEM: that is a different extension, with its own data collection rule.

Network security groups, and why application ones are preferred

A network security group is a list of rules with a numeric priority: they are evaluated lowest to highest and the first match decides, unlike conditional access. They carry default rules allowing traffic within the virtual network and blocking inbound from the internet, and those defaults sit at the highest priority numbers, so any rule of yours beats them.

An application security group is not a rule: it is a label applied to network interfaces. Rules are then written between groups — "from web to database, port 1433" — instead of between addresses. A new machine inherits its policy just by joining the group, and that is the reason they are preferred: the rule stops going stale every time the topology changes.

Azure Policy inheritance, which is where "management group" comes from

Policy assignments are inherited downwards through the hierarchy — management group, subscription, resource group, resource — and cannot be overridden from below. The effects matter in this order: Disabled turns the rule off; Deny prevents creating or modifying the resource; Audit only flags; and DeployIfNotExists and Modify remediate after deployment, for which the assignment needs a managed identity with permissions — a detail the exam uses as a distractor.

Since inheritance flows downwards and new subscriptions are born inside the hierarchy, an assignment at the management group reaches what does not exist yet. That is the whole landing zone argument.

Who is in charge of a cluster: two different RBACs

Two permission systems coexist in AKS and confusing them is a classic. Azure RBAC governs the cluster resource in Azure: who can read its configuration, scale it or download credentials. Kubernetes RBAC governs what is inside: who can list pods or read secrets in a namespace.

Entra ID integration joins the two worlds: the API server accepts an Entra ID token instead of a certificate from the kubeconfig file, so MFA, conditional access and PIM apply to the person; and Azure RBAC for Kubernetes additionally lets you assign the inside roles from Azure. Without that, a kubeconfig is a credential outside the directory: it is not revoked, it does not expire and it leaves no trace of who is who.

Service endpoint versus private endpoint

The most repeated failure: the private endpoint is created, public access is disabled and nobody links the DNS zone. It works from the portal and fails from the machine.

The product map, which is what actually gets asked

This domain has a lot of proper nouns and the exam sets them against each other. Before the detail it is worth pinning down which question each one answers, because nearly every mistake comes from answering with the product that answers a different question.

Posture is not detection The distinction most exploited in this domain: posture looks at configuration and says what could happen; workload protection looks at behaviour and says what is happening. A stem talking about recommendations, benchmark or compliance is asking for posture; one talking about an alert, suspicious activity or an incident is asking for a Defender plan.

Defender for Cloud and the Microsoft Cloud Security Benchmark (3.1.1)

Defender for Cloud is two products under one name, and knowing which one the stem is asking about settles half the questions in this task:

  • CSPM — posture management — continuously evaluates resource configuration against a standard and returns prioritised recommendations. Its foundational layer is on by default and free across subscriptions; Defender CSPM is the paid plan that adds agentless scanning, attack paths, the cloud security graph and governance over the recommendations.
  • CWPP — workload protection — is the set of plans per resource type: servers, storage, SQL, containers, App Service, Key Vault, APIs, AI services. These are what detect and alert.

The default standard is the Microsoft Cloud Security Benchmark: the security controls Microsoft considers the baseline for any cloud, mapped to CIS and NIST, and applied not just to Azure but to connected AWS and GCP accounts too. On top of it you can add regulatory standards — ISO 27001, PCI DSS, NIST — and your own.

The MCSB isn't chosen, it's extended A stem asking for "a security baseline across the whole cloud with minimal effort" is asking for the MCSB, which is already assigned. Writing your own Azure Policy initiative from scratch for that is the expensive answer, and it is usually the distractor.

The two Secure Scores, which are not the same (3.1.2)

Microsoft has two scores with almost identical names and the exam crosses them deliberately:

Microsoft Secure ScoreDefender for Cloud secure score
Where it livesThe Microsoft Defender portalDefender for Cloud, in Azure
What it measuresThe tenant's configuration: identity, email, apps, Intune-managed devices.The configuration of the resources in Azure, AWS and GCP.

A score is not a compliance report Neither of them demonstrates conformance with a regulation. A committee asking for "the degree of ISO 27001 compliance, control by control" needs the regulatory compliance dashboard. Confusing relative improvement with conformance is one of the most repeated errors on this exam.

Integrated posture in hybrid and multicloud environments (3.1.3)

The question almost always arrives the same way: "we have Azure, we have AWS, we have servers in our data centre, and we want to see it all in one place". The design has three pieces and each one covers a different gap:

What lives outside AzureHow it comes in
AWS accounts or GCP projectsDefender for Cloud's multicloud connectors, which read the other cloud's configuration agentlessly and apply the MCSB there too.
On-premises or other-cloud servers, as resourcesAzure Arc, which projects them into the Azure control plane so Policy, Machine Configuration and the Defender plans can apply.
Kubernetes outside AzureArc-enabled Kubernetes, which enables Defender for Containers and Azure Policy over other people's clusters.
Connector for the cloud, Arc for the machine This is the most confused pair. The AWS or GCP connector gives posture over the account and its services with nothing installed. Arc is for treating a machine or a cluster as an Azure resource. A scenario with AWS accounts and your own servers carries both.

Choosing the workload protection plans (3.1.4)

Plans are enabled per resource type and per subscription, and the exam wants you to pick the one that matches the risk the stem describes, not all of them just in case:

PlanWhat it brings
Defender for Servers P1Integrates Defender for Endpoint on the server: EDR, detection and response at the operating system.
Defender for Servers P2Everything in P1 plus agentless vulnerability and malware scanning, just-in-time access to the management ports, and file integrity monitoring.
Defender for StorageMalware scanning on upload, data sensitivity analysis and alerts on anomalous access.
Defender for ContainersImage scanning in the registry, runtime detection on the nodes and cluster posture.
Defender for SQL / App Service / Key Vault / Resource ManagerService-specific detection: SQL injection, code execution in the application, anomalous vault access, suspicious control-plane operations.
Defender for APIsInventory, posture and detection over APIs published in API Management.
The ports tell "Administrators need RDP or SSH, but we don't want the port permanently open" is just-in-time, and that lives in Defender for Servers P2. If the stem also says "without deploying agents on every machine", the tell points at agentless scanning in the same plan.

Azure Arc as a single control plane (3.1.5)

Arc is not a security agent: it is the piece that makes a server in your data centre, a machine in AWS or somebody else's Kubernetes cluster exist as Azure resources. Once projected, the same things apply to them as to a native resource:

  • Azure Policy and Machine Configuration to enforce and audit operating system configuration.
  • The Defender for Cloud plans, with Defender for Endpoint deployed as an extension.
  • Tags, RBAC and management groups, so governance is a single thing.
  • Extensions to take their logs into Log Analytics and, from there, into Sentinel.
Governing and logging are two decisions Arc governs the resource; getting its events into the SIEM additionally needs the Azure Monitor agent extension and its data collection rule. A stem asking for both needs both pieces in the answer.

Defender External Attack Surface Management (3.1.6)

EASM looks at your organisation from the outside, the way an attacker would. From a set of seeds — domains, IP ranges, organisation names — it discovers and maintains an inventory of what is published on the internet and belongs to you: domains and subdomains, hosts, certificates, pages and IP addresses, along with the problems it finds on them.

The word that gives it away EASM's value is in what you did not know you had: the subdomain from a forgotten campaign, the test environment left published, the about-to-expire certificate on a service nobody claims. If the stem says "unknown assets", "exposed shadow IT" or "what an attacker sees from the internet", it is EASM. Defender for Cloud only sees what is in the subscriptions you connected to it.

Security Exposure Management: from list to priority (3.1.7)

The problem it solves is not finding problems: it is that there are too many. Microsoft Security Exposure Management joins the signal from Defender XDR and Defender for Cloud into a graph and answers "where would they actually get in?".

PieceWhat it brings to the process
Attack pathsChained routes — from an exposed asset to a critical one — with their chokepoints. Fixing one chokepoint cuts many paths at once, and that is the prioritisation criterion the exam expects.
Attack surface map and reductionSeeing the estate as a graph of assets and relationships, and measuring what shrinks when you close each thing.
InitiativesSecurity programmes with metrics — ransomware, identity protection, SaaS security — so progress is tracked against a goal rather than a list.
Critical assetsMarking which resources genuinely matter, so prioritisation weighs impact and not only technical severity.
Severity is not priority Ten critical vulnerabilities on isolated machines matter less than one medium on the road to a domain controller. If the stem asks to "prioritise by real business risk", the answer is attack paths, not sorting by CVSS.

Requirements for servers, across platforms (3.2.1)

What you are expected to be able to specify, and what recurs scenario after scenario:

  • EDR on all of them: Defender for Endpoint covers Windows Server and Linux, and on servers it is deployed through Defender for Servers.
  • An operating system baseline applied and audited with Azure Policy and Machine Configuration, including Arc-connected machines.
  • Vulnerability and patch management, with Azure Update Manager for the update cycle.
  • No credentials on the machine: managed identities for anything consuming Azure services.
  • Bounded administrative access: no public IP, with Bastion or just-in-time, and from dedicated workstations.
  • Disk encryption and, where the regulation demands it, customer-managed keys.

Mobile devices and client machines (3.2.2)

BYOD isn't enrolled A stem saying "the employee uses their own phone and the company cannot manage it" is ruling out enrolment. The answer is app protection policies, which protect the data without touching the device. Requiring enrolment there is the distractor.

IoT and embedded systems (3.2.3)

The design principle: an IoT device is just another machine, with less ability to defend itself. What gets specified:

  • A per-device identity — an X.509 certificate or TPM, never one key shared across all of them — provisioned with Azure IoT Hub's Device Provisioning Service.
  • Remote, signed firmware updates, because a device that cannot be patched is permanent debt.
  • Network segmentation: devices talk to their hub and to nothing else.
  • Secure boot and hardware key storage on embedded devices that support it.
  • For office IoT — cameras, printers, controllers — discovery goes through Enterprise IoT, integrated into Defender for Endpoint.

Operational technology and ICS with Defender for IoT (3.2.4)

The plant network has one constraint that changes everything: you cannot install anything on the devices and you cannot interrupt the process. That is why Defender for IoT works with a passive network sensor on a mirror port: it listens to the traffic, identifies the industrial protocols — Modbus, DNP3, S7, EtherNet/IP — and from there produces the asset inventory, the vulnerabilities and the anomalies, without sending a single packet.

How the scenario is recognised "Production plant", "PLC", "we can't install agents", "the line can't be stopped". The answer is Defender for IoT with OT sensors, and the MITRE ATT&CK for ICS matrix is the one used to evaluate coverage. Defender for Endpoint does not come in: there is nowhere to install it.

The rollout pattern is the same as in governance: audit first to measure the real scope, remediate what exists, and only then enforce, so you do not break what already works.

Windows LAPS (3.2.6)

One local administrator password repeated across the whole estate is the classic lateral-movement enabler: one compromised machine opens all the others. Windows LAPS is built into the operating system, generates a different password per machine, rotates it by itself and stores the copy in Entra ID or AD DS, with scoped, audited read permission.

What does not work as an alternative Renaming the account does not stop the hash being replayed. A different password per department set by group policy is still shared — and visible in the policy itself. And disabling the local account strands the machine the day it loses connectivity to the domain.

Baselines for SaaS, PaaS and IaaS (3.3.1)

The same idea applied to managed services: write down which configuration is acceptable and make it verifiable. For Azure services the MCSB already carries that baseline and Defender for Cloud evaluates it on its own; what you add is whatever the organisation decides on top, with Azure Policy initiatives assigned to a management group. For SaaS — third-party applications nobody configures from Azure — the baseline is evaluated and applied from Defender for Cloud Apps, with its SaaS security posture management.

Three surfaces, three tools IaaS and PaaS: Defender for Cloud and Azure Policy. SaaS: Defender for Cloud Apps. Microsoft 365 tenant configuration: Secure Score and each workload's own policies. Answering with another surface's tool is the most common mistake in this task.

Requirements for IoT workloads (3.3.2)

Here it is no longer about the device but about the platform receiving it. What gets specified: per-device authentication against IoT Hub with certificates, least privilege per device, private endpoints so the hub is not exposed to the internet, encryption in transit and at rest, and separation between the data plane — telemetry — and the management plane. And what the design usually misses: how a compromised device is revoked, which is where having an individual identity rather than a common key pays off.

Requirements for web workloads (3.3.3)

Front Door or Application Gateway Both carry a WAF. Front Door is global and comes in when the stem mentions several regions, worldwide latency or failover between regions. Application Gateway is regional, inside the virtual network, and comes in when traffic must not leave it.

Requirements for containers (3.3.4)

The exam walks the whole lifecycle and expects a control at each stage:

  • Image — vulnerability scanning in the registry and in the pipeline, minimal signed base images, and a private registry accessed by managed identity.
  • Deployment — admission control that rejects what is non-compliant: Azure Policy for Kubernetes, which turns away privileged containers, host mounts or images from unapproved registries.
  • Runtime — Defender for Containers, with detection on the node and on the cluster's control plane.
  • Secrets — never in the image or in environment variables: Key Vault with the CSI driver and a workload identity.

Securing the Azure AI services (3.3.6)

New syllabus content and highly askable, because it combines things you already know with two risks that did not exist before. An AI service is protected across four planes:

PlaneWhat gets specified
AccessDisable the resource's local keys and authenticate with Entra ID and managed identities, with RBAC by service role.
NetworkPrivate endpoint and public access disabled, so the model is only reachable from the application's network.
DataCustomer-managed keys, control over what is written to the traces, and knowing which data leaves in the prompt and which stays.
Content and abuseContent filters and prompt shields against direct and indirect instruction injection, and the Defender for Cloud plan for AI services to detect abuse at runtime.
The risk specific to AI Prompt injection — content the model reads ending up directing what it does — is not solved by any network or identity measure, and that is exactly the point of the question. It is answered with the service's own filters and shields, and by bounding what the model can do with the identity it is given.

Evaluating a network design (3.4.1)

You are rarely asked to design the network from scratch; you are asked to look at one and say what is wrong. The checklist:

  • Segmentation — hub and spoke, with workloads separated by trust rather than by owning team.
  • Egress inspection — Azure Firewall as the controlled exit point; the Premium tier adds IDPS and TLS inspection when the requirement calls for it.
  • Fine-grained filtering — network security groups backed by application security groups, so rules speak of roles and not addresses.
  • PaaS off the internet — private endpoints and public access disabled; a service endpoint keeps traffic on the Azure backbone but does not give the service a private IP.
  • Edge — WAF and DDoS in front of anything published.
  • No direct hop — no public IP and no published RDP: Bastion and just-in-time.
  • Monitoring — the fourth technique alongside segmenting, filtering and posture, and the one most often forgotten: virtual network flow logs to know who talks to whom, Network Watcher with its IP flow verify and next hop diagnostics to understand why a rule blocks, and connection monitor to watch continuously that a critical path stays open. Without these, a network design can be neither debugged nor audited.

Entra Internet Access as a secure web gateway (3.4.2)

This is the Security Service Edge piece for outbound traffic. It replaces the data centre proxy: the Global Secure Access client forwards browsing to the Microsoft service, where web content filtering by category and name is applied, along with inspection and — the thing that separates it from a classic proxy — the same conditional access that already governs applications, with the user's identity and their device state as signals.

The advantage they want you to name The policy follows the user. It makes no difference whether they are in the office, at home or in an airport: it does not depend on traffic passing through the corporate network, and that is why it solves the hybrid work scenario without backhauling traffic to the data centre.

Microsoft traffic and cross-tenant scenarios (3.4.3)

The Microsoft traffic forwarding profile handles traffic to Microsoft 365 and the Entra services, and it enables two controls the exam asks about by name:

  • Compliant network — a conditional access condition requiring the request to arrive through the service, so a stolen token is useless outside it. It is stronger than an IP-range condition, because it cannot be faked by changing networks.
  • Tenant restrictions v2 — stopping a user, from a corporate device, signing in to another tenant with personal or third-party credentials. It is the control for the "I don't want data leaving here into their own OneDrive" scenario.

Entra Private Access (3.4.4)

The other half of SSE: access to what is internal. It publishes private applications — web and non-web, by port and protocol — through connectors installed next to them, with nothing opened to the outside and without giving the user an address on the corporate network. Each application is its own resource, with its own conditional access policy, which is what makes it zero trust access rather than a VPN.

Classic VPNEntra Private Access
What it grantsAccess to the network; from there you reach whatever the network reaches.Access to one application, evaluated on every session.
What is publishedA concentrator exposed to the internet.Nothing: the connector opens the connection outbound.

Common traps in this domain

Confusing the two Secure Scores The one in the Defender portal measures the tenant; the one in Defender for Cloud measures the resources. And neither is a regulatory compliance report.
Arc where a multicloud connector belonged Arc projects machines and clusters. The posture of an AWS or GCP account comes in through the Defender for Cloud connector, agentlessly.
Agents on the OT network On the plant floor nothing is installed and the process is not interrupted: a passive Defender for IoT sensor on a mirror port.
Enrolling the personal phone If the stem says the company does not manage the device, the answer is app protection policies, not MDM.
Service endpoint instead of private endpoint A service endpoint keeps traffic on the Azure backbone, but the resource still has a public address. If the requirement is "only reachable from the virtual network", it is a private endpoint with public access disabled.
Prioritising by severity What this exam asks for is cutting attack paths towards critical assets, not emptying the findings list in CVSS order.
